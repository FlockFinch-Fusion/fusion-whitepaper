\section{Learning to Allocate Intelligence}
\label{sec:learning}

The visible orchestration graph is not the durable advantage of \systemname.
An asymmetric pool can be copied. The harder object to reproduce is an
improving empirical model of how intelligence should be allocated across real
tasks.

\subsection{A conditional capability map}

The capability map estimates more than whether a model answers a benchmark item
correctly. Its object is the model--task interaction under a particular role
and system state. A useful record includes model and provider version, role,
context view, task dimensions, evidence already present, output validity,
latency, cost, final decision, and observed outcome.

This map must be versioned and uncertainty-aware. Model populations are small,
non-stationary, and often clustered by training lineage; benchmark items are
not the production task distribution. A compact latent ability space can be a
useful estimator, but not a timeless ontology of model intelligence. New models
should enter through targeted probes and online calibration rather than a
hand-authored global ranking.

\subsection{The allocation flywheel}

Each completed task can generate a learning tuple
\begin{equation}
 (S_t, E_t, a_t, Y_{a_t}, d_t, o_t, C_t, L_t),
\end{equation}
where $d_t$ is the committed decision and $o_t$ is the eventual outcome. Across
tasks, these tuples improve task-state representation, conditional capability
estimates, call admission, role assignment, and stopping.

The flywheel has two inputs. Producer behavior supplies observations about what
different models can contribute. Synthesizer decisions and downstream outcomes
supply feedback about which contributions were useful. This creates a natural
route toward reinforcement learning from model and environment feedback, but
the training target is allocation policy rather than imitation of a single
model's answer.

Logged production data alone is not enough. The policy observes outcomes only
for calls it chose to make, producing selection bias. A small budget for
randomized exploration, shadow calls, candidate-subset ablations, and offline
replay is required to estimate counterfactual marginal value. Credit assignment
must also be local: one successful trajectory should not reward every call in
it equally, and one failure should not condemn every model that contributed.

\subsection{From typed policy to learned policy}

The current architecture exposes a stable learning interface:
\begin{align}
 &(\text{task state},\text{evidence},\text{supply}) \notag\\
 &\hspace{2.2em}\longrightarrow
 (\text{call},\text{role},\text{budget},\text{stop}).
\end{align}
Typed perceptions and deterministic policy boundaries provide interpretable
guardrails while outcome data is limited. An end-to-end allocator can gradually
learn within this action space, first predicting existing policy decisions,
then optimizing task outcome under cost and risk constraints.

\subsection{Bounded system-level recursive improvement}

This process is recursive in a bounded, system-level sense. \systemname uses
traces of its own behavior to improve how future intelligence is allocated.
It does not imply unrestricted self-modification, recursive weight rewriting,
or an unobservable optimization loop. Improvements remain versioned, evaluated,
and reversible.

The result is a compound intelligence system driven by two forms of progress:
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{\text{better models}}_{\text{supply-side improvement}}
 \quad\times\quad
 \underbrace{\text{better allocation}}_{\text{demand-side improvement}}.
\end{equation}
When a worker model improves, \systemname inherits a stronger evidence source.
When the allocation policy improves, it extracts more value from the entire
existing pool. Neither engine requires the other to stand still.
