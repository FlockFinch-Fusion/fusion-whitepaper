\section{The Intelligence Allocation Gap}

AI is moving from a world of scarce, general-purpose model intelligence to an
ecosystem of abundant and heterogeneous capability. Frontier models continue
to advance, while smaller and specialized models increasingly offer distinct
strengths in reasoning, code, scientific domains, critique, tool use, speed,
and deployment economics. Intelligence is no longer contained in one obvious
place.

The way applications consume intelligence has not kept pace with this change.
A model is typically chosen when an application is designed, attached to a
prompt or agent loop, and treated as the intelligence of the system. The model
may be upgraded, or a simple router may direct an entire request elsewhere,
but the basic contract remains static: one task is assigned to one model-shaped
capability profile.

Real tasks are not static. The capability they require changes as the work
unfolds. A problem may begin with broad exploration, narrow to a precise
calculation, require an external observation, expose a risky assumption, and
end with a consequential decision. The best source of help at one moment may
be redundant at the next. A stronger model is not always the missing
ingredient; sometimes the missing ingredient is an independent critique, a
specialized fact, a test result, or simply the judgment to stop computing.

This is the \emph{intelligence allocation gap}: the distance between the
capabilities available across the model ecosystem and the capabilities a task
can actually draw upon, in the right role, at the right moment.

The gap has two visible costs. The first is lost capability. A fixed model
cannot consistently reproduce the complementary strengths of the entire
ecosystem, and repeated reasoning from one perspective can leave the same blind
spot intact. The second is wasted compute. When a system lacks a model of what
the task needs, it spends expensive intelligence on work that is already
resolved, structurally redundant, or better performed elsewhere.

These are not separate product problems. They are the same systems problem.
Both arise because the application does not understand how to match an evolving
demand for intelligence with a heterogeneous supply of it.

\subsection{From a model to a compound intelligence system}

\systemname is Fumo Lab's answer to this gap. It introduces \emph{allocation
intelligence}: the system-level ability to interpret the current task state,
determine which uncertainty matters next, select the capability most likely to
resolve it, and decide when another computation is no longer valuable.

This changes the unit of intelligence presented to the application. The
application no longer depends directly on the strengths and weaknesses of a
single model. It interacts with one coherent system that can draw on many
models without exposing their coordination burden. Internally, models can
solve, explore, challenge, or verify. Externally, Fusion preserves one
conversation, one tool protocol, and one accountable path from evidence to
action.

Fusion is therefore not a conventional ensemble and not a cheaper substitute
for a frontier model. It is a \emph{compound intelligence system}: frontier
judgment, specialized capability, efficient computation, and task-state
understanding become parts of one adaptive decision process.

\subsection{The opportunity}

The long-term opportunity is larger than selecting better models for current
tasks. Model progress expands the supply of intelligence available to the
system. Allocation progress improves the system's ability to convert that
supply into outcomes. When the two improve together, the system can advance
along a dimension that neither better base models nor more inference calls can
create alone.

This whitepaper presents that vision, the design philosophy required to make it
coherent, the system Fumo Lab is building toward it, and the evidence by
which the vision should be judged.
